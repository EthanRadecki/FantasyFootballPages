Championship Gauntlet
Who had to earn it?
How It Works

Each title run is scored on three things: points opponents actually scored against the champion, how strong those opponents were across the regular season, and whether they were hot or cold in the five games right before facing the champion. Every score is measured against the full history of the league, so the scale stays fixed as new champions get added. Three-game and four-game runs get a small statistical adjustment to compare fairly. Weighted 70/15/15, heavily toward how tough the games themselves actually were.

Opp. Points Against (70%)
Opp. Dominance (15%)
Opp. Hot Streak (15%)

Gauntlet Score blends three z-scored components: points scored against the champion, opponent regular-season dominance, and opponent hot streak entering the game. Each is measured against the full league history and mapped through a fixed curve, not scaled to just these six runs. Games are lightly shrunk toward the league average based on how many rounds each champion played, so three-game and four-game runs compare fairly. Weighted 70/15/15, so this is mostly about how tough the actual games were, with dominance and hot streak adding a smaller layer of nuance. A score near 50 is a typical path. Anything further out means the field was tougher or softer than usual.

How hard is hard? The same math, every 3-week stretch ever.

A title run is really just a 3-game (or 4-game) stretch of opponents. The same Gauntlet Score methodology applies to any manager's any 3 consecutive real weeks, not just playoff runs. Applied to all 575 such stretches in league history (one known forfeit week excluded), deduplicated to one stretch per manager-season, it reveals the true scale: even the single hardest stretch ever only reaches 75.2, because averaging three games' worth of opponent quality mechanically pulls the result toward the middle. That compression is what makes the scale honest, and it's why a title run's score can look modest in isolation while still ranking among the toughest ever.

Schedule Luck
Who has the schedule been kind to?
How It Works

Each week, every manager's score is compared to the league median. If you scored above the median, you "should have" won that week regardless of your actual opponent. The difference between your actual wins and these expected wins is your schedule luck. Positive means the schedule gifted you wins; negative means it took them away.

Seasonal Analysis
Which quarter of the season matters most?
Methodology

A logistic regression tested which part of the season best predicts playoff qualification, using average points scored each quarter as independent variables. The model achieved an AUC of 0.83, indicating strong predictive accuracy. Q2 (Weeks 4-6) and Q4 (Weeks 10-13) carry the largest coefficients, while Q3 (Weeks 7-9) is the weakest predictor.

Positional Production
Which positions actually win you games?
Methodology

Average weekly points come from started lineups only, across every regular season game and every real playoff bracket game. Consolation-bracket games are excluded entirely, same as the rest of the site. A season-level regression (n=83 manager-seasons, R²=0.57) tests how much each position's average weekly production predicts that season's win%.

Win% Attribution
Where does a season's win% actually come from?
Methodology

Five ingredients, each pulled from the metric that already powers its own page: draft surplus (Surplus Value), waiver upside (only the positive value from pickups counts, bad and neutral adds contribute nothing), missed wins from Lineup Efficiency (a bad start/sit call only counts if it actually flipped a real result), trade QUAD (Trade Value), and schedule luck (regular season only, real bracket games excluded same as consolation weeks). The outcome being explained, Win%, includes real playoff bracket games (still no consolation games), which schedule luck itself deliberately doesn't: a shrinking bracket field breaks the weekly-median comparison luck depends on in a way it doesn't break a plain win/loss count. A season-level regression (n=83 manager-seasons, R²=0.44) turns these into a per-manager breakdown of exactly how many points of win%, above or below league average, each ingredient is responsible for.

Each step is that ingredient's real contribution, in percentage points, relative to a league-average manager. The last two bars show the model's full prediction against the manager's actual win% — the gap between them is what the five ingredients don't explain.

Draft skill is the single biggest lever, with missed wins from lineup mistakes close behind. Waiver value edges out trade quality and schedule luck, which land in a near tie for last. Waiver activity only shows up once it's measured as pure upside, ignore the churn and the misses, and count only the real hits. Even together, these five ingredients explain 44% of season-to-season win% variance, leaving well over half of what separates a good season from a bad one to real scoring production and variance the roster-building decisions above don't capture.
